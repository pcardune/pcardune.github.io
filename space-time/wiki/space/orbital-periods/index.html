Orbital periods

Why planets close to their star have short years, and distant ones have very long ones.

A planet's orbital period is the length of its year, the time it takes to go round its star once. It follows a simple rule, found by Johannes Kepler in 1619: the farther out a planet is, the longer its year, and the relationship is exact.

Kepler's third law says that the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the distance. For a star like the Sun, if you measure the distance in AU and the time in years, the two numbers match: a planet at 4 AU has a period of 8 years (see Astronomical units and light-years).

Distance isn't the only thing that matters. A more massive star pulls harder, so planets circle it faster. A dim red dwarf has a habitable zone so close in that its planets can have years of only a few days.

A planet also moves faster the closer it is. Mercury travels at about 47 km/s, and Neptune at only 5.

This is why planets in the same system have such different years. Mercury's lasts 88 Earth days and Neptune's 165 Earth years. Moons follow the same law around their planets, and so do spacecraft (see Gravity assist).

In Space Time, a planet's year is shown after it has been surveyed.
