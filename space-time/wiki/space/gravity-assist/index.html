Gravity assist

How a spacecraft borrows speed and direction from a passing planet or star.

A gravity assist, or slingshot, lets a spacecraft change speed and direction by flying past a planet, with no fuel at all. The planet's gravity bends the spacecraft's path, and because the planet is itself moving round the Sun, the spacecraft can gain some of its speed.

Think of a tennis ball thrown at a moving train. It bounces off faster than it was thrown, because it picks up some of the train's speed. A planet does the same for a spacecraft passing close to it. The planet is slowed by an unnoticeable amount, since it is so much heavier.

It makes impossible missions possible. Voyager 2 used gravity assists at Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus to reach Neptune, and each one added speed and turned the probe toward the next planet. The Cassini mission flew past Venus twice, then Earth and then Jupiter, to reach Saturn. A rocket large enough to go there directly would have been impractical.

The same idea works in reverse. A flyby can slow a craft down, so one headed for the inner solar system, like the Parker Solar Probe, uses Venus to shed speed.

In Space Time, the gravity of every nearby star bends a solar probe's path, and heavier stars bend it more.
