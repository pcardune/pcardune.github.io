Astronomical units and light-years

The two yardsticks astronomers use for distances inside a planetary system and between stars.

Kilometers are too small to be useful for space. The distance from Earth to the Sun is about 150 million of them. So astronomers use bigger units.

The astronomical unit, or AU, is the average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometers. It is the yardstick for distances inside a planetary system. Mars orbits at about 1.5 AU, Jupiter at 5.2 and Neptune at 30. A star's habitable zone and frost line are usually given in AU.

Between the stars, even AU is too small. The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 270,000 AU away. So astronomers use the light-year, the distance light travels in a year. That is about 9.5 trillion kilometers, or about 63,000 AU. Proxima Centauri is 4.2 light-years away, and the center of our galaxy is about 26,000.

A light-year measures distance, not time, though it does mean something about time. The light we see from a star 100 light-years away left it 100 years ago.

In Space Time, distances inside a star system are shown in AU.
