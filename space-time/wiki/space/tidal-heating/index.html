Tidal heating

How the gravity of a giant planet can heat a moon from the inside.

A moon is stretched a little by the gravity of the planet it orbits, because the pull is stronger on the near side than on the far side (see Tidal locking). If the moon's orbit is a perfect circle, it is held in one stretched shape and nothing more happens. But if the orbit is slightly oval, the moon is stretched more when it is close and less when it is far, and it flexes in and out on every orbit.

All that flexing makes friction inside the moon, the same way that bending a paperclip back and forth makes it warm. This is tidal heating. It can be a lot of heat. It is the reason Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanic world in the solar system (see Volcanic moons).

Moons keep their orbits oval because they tug on each other. Io, Europa and Ganymede circle Jupiter in a resonance, with Io going round four times for each time Ganymede goes round once, so their pulls keep repeating and keeping the orbits from becoming circular.

It also heats colder moons. Under Europa's ice and Saturn's moon Enceladus, tidal heating keeps water liquid far from the Sun (see Icy moons).

In Space Time, tidal heating is why the innermost moons of giants can be volcanic.
