Day and year

How long a world's day and year are, and why they differ so much.

A day is how long a world takes to spin once. A year is how long it takes to orbit its star once. The two are unrelated. Earth's day is 24 hours and its year about 365 days, but other worlds mix them up.

Venus spins so slowly that its day, about 243 Earth days, is longer than its year of 225. Jupiter rotates in about ten hours, so fast that its clouds are stretched into bands. Mercury turns exactly three times for every two orbits, which makes a sunrise-to-sunrise day last twice as long as its year.

Year length follows distance. A planet close to its star moves fast on a short orbit, and one far away takes much longer (see Orbital periods).

Day length shapes climate. A long day gives the Sun time to bake one side and let the other freeze. A short day spreads the heat around and drives strong winds. Fast rotation also helps make a magnetic field, while a world that locks to its star stops having days at all.

In Space Time, the day and year are shown for a surveyed planet. A locked world shows no day length.
