Axial tilt

Why a tilted planet has seasons.

A planet's axial tilt is the angle between its spin axis and the plane of its orbit. Earth's is about 23°, which is why we have seasons. When the northern half leans toward the Sun, it gets longer, more direct days and warms up. Six months later it leans away. Seasons come from tilt, not from distance. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.

With no tilt there would be no seasons, only a steady climate, hot at the equator and cold at the poles. A world with an extreme tilt has extreme seasons. Uranus is tipped on its side at about 98°, so each pole gets roughly 42 years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 years of darkness.

Tilt can change. Mars is tilted much like Earth now, but its tilt has swung widely over millions of years, because nothing steadies it. Earth is held in check by the Moon, whose gravity keeps the tilt within a narrow range, which has kept our climate stable over long ages.

In Space Time, a rocky world with a moon keeps a moderate tilt, and tidally locked worlds are barely tilted at all. A steeper tilt widens a world's temperature range.
