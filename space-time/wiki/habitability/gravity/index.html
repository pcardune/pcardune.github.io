Gravity

How strongly a world pulls, and why that decides whether it can keep its air.

A world's surface gravity comes from how much mass it has and how close you stand to the center. A bigger world pulls harder, but a puffed-up one pulls less, because the surface is farther out. Gravity is usually given in g, with Earth as 1. Mars is about 0.4 g and the Moon about 0.17 g.

Gravity matters for air. A weak pull can't hold molecules down, so small worlds lose their atmosphere to space (see Escape velocity and Atmospheric escape). That is one reason the Moon is airless and Earth is not.

Gravity also matters for bodies. Low gravity weakens bones and muscles over time, as astronauts find. Heavy worlds are harder. Hearts, bones and joints all work against the extra pull, and a heavy world holds a thicker atmosphere that traps more heat.

Giant planets have no surface to stand on. Their gravity is measured at the cloud tops, where Jupiter's is about two and a half times Earth's.

In Space Time, a world's gravity is shown in g.
