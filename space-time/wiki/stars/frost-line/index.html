Frost line

The distance from a star beyond which water freezes, and giants form.

In the cloud of gas and dust that forms a new star and its planets, some materials freeze solid and others stay gas. The frost line, or snow line, is the distance from the star beyond which water turns to ice. Close in, only rock and metal can be solid. Past the line, ice is solid too, and there is a lot more of it.

That extra material changes what planets can grow. Beyond the line, a planet can pile up a large icy core quickly, and a big core pulls in the surrounding hydrogen and helium to become a giant. This is why Jupiter and Saturn lie beyond the frost line in our own system, while the small rocky planets are all inside it. Even farther out, where it is colder still, come the ice giants Uranus and Neptune.

The frost line moves with the star. A bright star pushes it far out, and a dim star pulls it in close. In the Sun's system it lies at about 2.7 AU, in the asteroid belt (see Astronomical units and light-years).

Not all planets stay where they formed. Some giants migrate inward and become hot Jupiters (see Gas giant).

In Space Time, rocky and dwarf planets mostly form inside the frost line, gas giants just beyond it, and ice giants farther out.
