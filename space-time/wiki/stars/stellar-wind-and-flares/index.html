Stellar wind and flares

What stars throw at their planets, and why it can strip a world's air.

Every star blows a constant stream of charged particles into space, called its stellar wind. The Sun's is the solar wind, and it reaches far past Neptune. Stars also have occasional flares, sudden bursts of light and particles from tangled magnetic fields near the surface.

For a planet, this is both a danger and an effect. The particles can knock gas out of the top of an atmosphere and carry it away, a little at a time, especially if the planet has no magnetic field to deflect them. A strong flare also sends a blast of ultraviolet light and X-rays that can damage living things.

Stars differ in how stormy they are. The Sun is fairly calm. Young stars of every kind are more active, and many red dwarfs stay violently active for billions of years. Because their habitable zones are so close in, their planets are hit especially hard. Massive blue stars blow far stronger winds than the Sun.

The solar wind is also what makes the aurora, when its particles are guided into the upper air at Earth's poles.

In Space Time, red dwarfs are treated as flaring stars, and worlds around them lose their air faster.
