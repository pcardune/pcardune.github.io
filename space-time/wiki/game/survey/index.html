Survey

How the ship learns what a world is really like, from orbit.

A planet you have only discovered is mostly a point on a map. You know where it is and how it orbits, and little else. A survey fills in the rest.

To survey a planet, the ship enters orbit and stays for a few seconds while its instruments measure the world. Leave early and the survey is lost. When it finishes, the planet's panel shows its mass and gravity, its temperature, the length of its day and year, its magnetic field, its geology, its atmosphere and water, and a verdict on how good a home it could be for life.

The ship never lands, so everything is learned from orbit. That is how real spacecraft work. Orbiters around Mars measure its gravity, map its magnetic patches and analyze its air from above.

Moons are studied differently. A probe flying past a moon analyzes it, which reveals its details.

Each planet surveyed also restocks the ship's probes.
