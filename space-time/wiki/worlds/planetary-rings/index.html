Planetary rings

Thin discs of ice and rock circling a planet, like Saturn's.

A ring is a flat disc of countless small pieces, from dust grains to boulders, all orbiting a planet. Saturn's are the famous ones. They are mostly water ice, and from Earth they look solid and shiny. Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune have rings too, but thin and faint, and some small bodies have rings as well.

Rings are wide but extremely thin. Saturn's main rings stretch about 280,000 kilometers across, roughly three-quarters of the way from Earth to the Moon, yet are often only about ten meters thick. That is far thinner, in proportion, than a sheet of paper.

Where they come from is still debated. Some may be the remains of a moon that strayed too close and was pulled apart by the planet's gravity. Others may be leftovers of the disc that formed the planet's moons. Saturn's rings may be young, perhaps only a few hundred million years old, so we may be lucky to see them.

Rings don't last forever. They slowly spread, and material falls onto the planet.

In Space Time, about a third of gas giants have rings, and some ice giants have thin ones.
