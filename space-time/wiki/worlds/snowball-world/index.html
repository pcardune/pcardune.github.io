Snowball world

A planet frozen over from pole to pole, and how ice keeps it that way.

A snowball world is a planet whose ocean has frozen over, or nearly so. Ice is white and reflects most sunlight, so a frozen planet absorbs much less of its star's warmth and stays cold (see Ice-albedo feedback). A world can stay locked that way even with a light that would have kept it warm if it had been ice-free.

Earth has probably been through it. Around 700 million years ago, in the Cryogenian period, evidence from glacial deposits near the equator suggests that ice covered most or all of the planet, perhaps more than once. Life survived, probably in cracks, in warm springs and under thin ice.

What ended it was volcanoes. With the surface sealed under ice, the carbon dioxide they release could not be absorbed by rock and ocean, so it built up in the air for millions of years. Eventually the greenhouse warming (Greenhouse effect) was enough to melt the ice. Once it began, the same feedback ran the other way, and the planet thawed quickly.

Frozen worlds may be common, since many planets sit near the cold edge of their star's habitable zone.

In Space Time, a world that has frozen over tends to stay that way, because its ice keeps reflecting the starlight.
