Equilibrium temperature

The temperature a planet settles at when it radiates as much heat as it absorbs.

Every planet absorbs starlight and gives off heat. If it takes in more than it gives out, it warms, and a warmer body radiates more. The equilibrium temperature is the point where the two are equal and the temperature stops changing.

It depends on three things: how bright the star is, how far away the planet is, and how much light it reflects (see Albedo). A bright star or a close orbit makes it hotter, and a bright, reflective planet makes it colder. Its air does not appear, because it is the temperature of a bare planet, as if it had no greenhouse effect.

For Earth, this works out to about -18 °C. The real surface is about 33 degrees warmer, and the difference comes from the greenhouse effect (see Greenhouse effect).

Astronomers use it to compare worlds they can't see in detail. For a distant planet they can often tell its orbit and its star but not its air, so equilibrium temperature is a quick estimate of how warm it is, and whether it lies in the habitable zone.

In Space Time, the temperature shown for a giant is its cloud-top temperature, which is found this way.
