Why climates change slowly

How oceans and ice hold back a planet's temperature, so changes take centuries to show.

When something changes a planet's climate, such as more greenhouse gas or a brighter star, the temperature doesn't jump to its new level. It drifts there, and the delay can be long.

The main reason is the ocean. Water takes a lot of heat to warm up, far more than rock or air, and the ocean is deep. It soaks up the extra warmth and releases it slowly, so the surface lags behind the push. On Earth, the full response to a change takes decades to centuries for the upper ocean, and far longer for the deep ocean.

Ice sheets are even slower. Melting a kilometer-thick ice cap takes thousands of years, and it can go on well after the temperature has changed.

This delay has consequences. A planet can be caught in the middle of a change, with its air already altered and its temperature not yet moved. It is also why a frozen world can stay frozen for so long (see Ice-albedo feedback).

For worlds with no ocean, such as a bare rock, the temperature follows the light almost at once, because there is nothing to hold the heat.

In Space Time, a world's temperature moves toward the balance its air sets with a lag of centuries, so the effect of a change builds up gradually.
