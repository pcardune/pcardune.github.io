Carbonate-silicate cycle

The slow thermostat that keeps a world's carbon dioxide in balance over millions of years.

Earth has a thermostat. Over hundreds of thousands of years, it keeps the climate from drifting too far, and it is called the carbonate-silicate cycle.

Volcanoes release carbon dioxide into the air. Rain absorbs some of it and turns slightly acidic, and that rain slowly wears down rocks made of silicate minerals. The dissolved remains wash to the sea, where they combine with the carbon into limestone and other carbonates on the seafloor. Plate tectonics then drags that seafloor under the continents, where heat releases the carbon dioxide again through volcanoes, and the loop closes.

The thermostat works because weathering speeds up when it is warm and wet. If the planet warms, more carbon dioxide is pulled out, which cools it. If it cools, weathering slows and volcanoes top up the carbon dioxide, which warms it. It also explains how Earth got out of its snowball phases (see Snowball world).

The cycle needs liquid water, to make rain, and a geologically active planet, to recycle the carbon. Planets without them, such as Mars, have no thermostat (see Tectonics and volcanism).

In Space Time, the cycle runs on geologically active worlds, and a dead world's carbon dioxide just stays in the air.
